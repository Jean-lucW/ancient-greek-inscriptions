**Part 1**

Literary version (Plut. Lives of the Ten Orators 852)

(851f) Lykophron son of Lykourgos of Boutadai registered that he was entitled to dining in the city hall in accordance with the award given

(852a) by the People to Lykourgos of Boutadai. In the archonship of Anaxikrates (307/6), in the sixth prytany, of Antiochis. Stratokles son of Euthydemos of Diomeia proposed: since Lykourgos son of Lykophron of Boutadai, having taken over from his own ancestors the good will towards the People which had belonged to his family from of old <...>, and the ancestors of Lykourgos, Lykomedes and Lykourgos, were both honoured by the People while they were living and, when they died, the People gave them public burials in the Kerameikos for their manly virtue, and Lykourgos himself,

(852b) engaging in politics, enacted many fine laws for his fatherland, and having been treasurer of the common revenue for the city for three four-year periods and having distributed from the common revenue eighteen thousand nine hundred talents, and having received much (money) from private individuals on trust and advanced it as loans both for the critical times of the city and of the People, in all six hundred and fifty talents, and having been judged to have administered all these things justly, he was crowned many times by the city; and further, having been elected by the People, he collected much money on the acropolis, and having provided cult equipment for the goddess, solid-gold Victories, gold and silver processional vessels and gold cult equipment

(852c) for a hundred basket-bearers; and having been elected in charge of the preparation for war, he brought up many weapons and fifty thousand missiles to the acropolis, and fitted out four hundred seaworthy triremes, repairing some and building others from the beginning; and in addition to these, having taken them over half-finished, he completed the shipsheds and the arsenal and the theatre of Dionysos, and he finished the Panathenaic stadium and built the gymnasium at the Lykeion, and he adorned the city with many other constructions; and when king Alexander, having

(852d) subjugated all Asia and claiming to give orders to all the Greeks collectively, demanded the surrender of Lykourgos as acting against him, the People did not hand him over <despite their> fear of Alexander; and having many times undergone audit for his political conduct in the city, which was free and democratic, he continued unconvicted and incorruptible for the whole time; so that all may know that (the People) both values most highly, while they are living, those who choose to engage in politics justly on behalf of the democracy and of freedom, and renders to them, when they have died, ever-remembered thanks,

(852e) for good fortune, the People shall decide to praise Lykourgos son of Lykophron of Boutadai for his excellence and justice, and the People shall stand a bronze statue of him in the Agora, except where the law forbids setting one up, and give dining in the city hall to the eldest of the descendants of Lykourgos at the time for all time; and all his decrees shall be valid, and the secretary of the People shall set them up on stone stelai and stand them on the acropolis near the dedications; and for inscribing the stelai the treasurer of the People shall give fifty drachmas from the People's fund for expenditure on decrees.
